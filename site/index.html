Do You Realize Your Reality Is Becoming Increasingly Private?

In 2035, AR advertising is not merely changing what we buy. It is changing whether we still see the same world.

JW

Written by Joseph Wang, Senior Future Tech Correspondent

Aug. 11, 2035  ·  9 min read

SHARE@↑

Two friends are waiting for the same bus on the same street.

One sees a three-story animated character leaning over the bus stop, inviting her into a nearby store. The other sees a floating advertisement for a travel service. A third person, standing only a few feet away, sees neither. His glasses have replaced the entire scene with navigation arrows and restaurant recommendations.

Physically, all three people are in the same place. Visually, they are not.

For most of human history, sharing a location meant sharing at least the basic appearance of that location. We might pay attention to different details or interpret them differently, but the building, the sign, and the billboard remained visible to everyone.

THE SHORT ANSWER

The algorithmic filter has left the screen.

  • AR has moved algorithmic filtering from screens into physical space.
  • People can stand together while receiving incompatible visual scenes.
  • A shared civic layer may need to become protected infrastructure.

The loss of a shared reality

Traditional advertising may be intrusive, but it is usually public. When a company places an advertisement on a billboard, everyone nearby can inspect it. People can photograph it, discuss it, criticize it, or complain about it.

An AR advertisement may exist for only one person.

A building could appear covered in a luxury brand campaign to one pedestrian, display a virtual game to another, and remain almost unchanged to someone else. Even when people look in exactly the same direction, their glasses may generate unrelated scenes.

“Do you see that?” no longer guarantees a useful answer.

Human communication becomes more complicated

Conversation often begins with the world around us. We comment on a poster, notice a strange object, recommend a store, or warn someone about an obstacle. These small interactions work because people generally assume that the surrounding scene is common to everyone.

AR weakens that foundation. When people discuss why a place looks busy, valuable, or important, their descriptions may no longer match—not because either person is mistaken, but because each has been shown a different environment.

Communication would require an additional step: identifying which parts of an experience are physical, which are digital, and whether the digital layer is shared. AR would therefore change more than how humans interact with technology. It would change how we establish a basic agreement about the world around us.

Public space becomes privately rendered

Streets, parks, stations, and public buildings are shared spaces. People may use and interpret them differently, but their physical appearance provides a common reference point.

Personalized AR transforms that reference point into a customizable interface. A blank wall is no longer necessarily blank. It may become an animated storefront, an interactive game, a product demonstration, or a personalized message. A public square may appear festive to one user, highly commercialized to another, and almost empty to a third.

The physical space remains public, but its digital appearance is privately generated.

01

Public space has always contained advertising. Conventional advertising can be publicly examined. Personalized AR advertising cannot easily be observed by anyone except its intended target.

This gives advertising platforms a new kind of power. They are no longer competing for space on a website or a phone screen. They are deciding what buildings mean, which businesses appear important, where attention should move, and which parts of the city deserve to be noticed.

The filter bubble enters the physical world

Algorithmic bubbles are not new. Two people can open the same social media app and receive completely different news, opinions, and recommendations.

Until recently, these bubbles were largely contained inside personal screens. We could look up, put the phone away, and return to a physical environment that had not been individually reorganized for us.

AR removes that separation.

If a person frequently interacts with sports content, the city may fill with virtual athletes, product promotions, and event suggestions. Someone interested in fashion may see the same streets covered with virtual clothing displays. A person who regularly visits certain restaurants may be guided past similar businesses while alternative options become less visually noticeable.

Personalization can make information feel relevant, but it can also create a feedback loop. The system shows people more of what they have previously noticed. They respond to those suggestions, producing new data that encourages the system to narrow their environment even further.

The online filter bubble would no longer be somewhere we go. It would follow us through everyday life.

Reality becomes harder to define

The physical world does not disappear when people put on AR glasses. Buildings still exist, buses still travel along real streets, and other people still occupy physical space. But everyday experience is no longer entirely physical.

When digital objects remain anchored to locations, respond naturally to lighting, and interact with real surfaces, users may stop consciously separating the two layers. A virtual advertisement attached to a building can become as familiar as the building itself.

This creates a more complicated definition of reality. Is reality only the physical structure underneath the digital layer? Does it include the personalized information a person sees every day? If a virtual object influences where people walk, what they buy, and how they remember a place, is it less real simply because others cannot see it?

Reality has always involved interpretation. Personalized AR adds something new: the environment itself is now being edited before interpretation begins.

The same city could divide into different social worlds

Personalization is not based only on interests. Advertising systems may also classify users according to income, neighborhood, occupation, purchasing history, and predicted economic value.

As a result, different social groups could receive very different visual versions of the same city. Users identified as high-value customers might see detailed cultural events, premium services, and polished interactive experiences. Others may receive louder promotions, urgent discounts, or repetitive advertisements designed to produce immediate responses.

Some stores, services, and opportunities could be made more visible to selected groups and less noticeable to others. The division would not appear as a physical barrier. It would exist inside the recommendation system.

ONE PHYSICAL PLACE

Three algorithmic versions

HIGH-VALUE PROFILE

Premium services, detailed experiences, curated destinations.

IMPULSE PROFILE

Urgent discounts, dynamic offers, attention-grabbing prompts.

LOW-ENGAGEMENT PROFILE

Fewer details, repeated messages, less visible alternatives.

Two people could walk along the same street while being directed toward different businesses, different information, and different opportunities. Over time, these invisible differences could reinforce existing social divisions.

The cognitive cost of a personalized world

The physical environment currently provides moments of visual rest. A blank wall can remain a blank wall. An empty street does not need to communicate anything.

In an advertising-driven AR environment, every unused surface becomes potential media space. Windows can display moving product demonstrations. Vehicles can carry personalized characters. Empty areas can be filled with recommendations. Advertisements may change as users move, look away, slow down, or show signs of interest.

There may be fewer opportunities to simply observe without being addressed.

Constant visual competition could contribute to mental fatigue, stress, and difficulty concentrating. It may also produce a more subtle form of uncertainty. If users know that content has been created specifically for them, they may begin to question why it appeared.

Why did the system show me this building?

Why did that person receive a different message?

Is this information important—or merely paid to appear?

When people cannot easily compare their visual environment with others, ordinary confirmation becomes harder. The inability to establish “We both saw the same thing” may contribute to isolation and distrust.

Shared reality may need to become a protected resource

AR platforms often describe personalization as a matter of convenience. Relevant information appears at the right place and time, while unwanted content is filtered out.

But a functioning society needs more than individually relevant information. It also needs common reference points.

Public signs, emergency instructions, transportation information, and civic announcements must remain consistent and recognizable. Commercial digital objects should be visibly identified, and users should be able to determine which parts of a scene are physical, shared, or personalized.

Most importantly, people need an easy way to compare views. A shared-view function could reveal which digital objects are visible to everyone and which exist only inside an individual profile.

THE BOTTOM LINE

The most important privacy question may be how much of the world our glasses are allowed to construct.

When every person sees a different version of the same street, communication becomes harder, public space becomes less public, and algorithms gain the power to shape not only what we think about but what appears to exist around us.

We may continue to live together physically while drifting apart visually.

In 2035, the question that matters is: “Are we still looking at the same world?”
